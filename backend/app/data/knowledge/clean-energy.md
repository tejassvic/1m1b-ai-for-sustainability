## Efficiency first

The cheapest unit of energy is the one that is never consumed. Insulation,
draught sealing, window glazing, efficient lighting and controls all reduce
demand before any generation decision is made, and they usually pay back faster
than supply-side investment. For an institution, the highest-return measures
are typically lighting, cooling set-points and the standby load of equipment
left running outside working hours.

## Where a building's electricity goes

In most buildings, cooling or heating dominates, followed by lighting, plug
loads, and water heating. Because cooling load scales with heat gain, shading,
roof treatment and ventilation strategy affect electricity use more than the
cooling equipment's rated efficiency does.

## On-site renewable generation

Rooftop solar is now the most common institutional option. Sizing should follow
the load profile: a system matched to daytime demand avoids export losses where
export tariffs are unfavourable. Maintenance matters — soiling and undetected
faults can silently remove a meaningful share of output, so monitoring is part
of the installation, not an extra.

## Storage and load shifting

Batteries, thermal storage and scheduling shift demand away from peak periods.
Even without storage, moving flexible loads — pumping, charging, laundry — to
the middle of the day raises the share of renewable generation actually used.

## Purchasing clean power

Where on-site generation is impractical, a renewable electricity tariff or
power purchase agreement is the main lever. Quality varies: some products
deliver new additional generation, others merely reallocate existing
attributes. Contracts should be assessed on additionality, not on labels.

## Honest accounting

Report the emissions factor you actually used, and whether it is location-based
(grid average) or market-based (contractual). Mixing the two — claiming a market
factor reduction while comparing against a location-based baseline — overstates
progress and is the most common error in organisational energy reporting.

## A note on fairness

Energy poverty is real in both rural and urban settings. Efficiency programmes
that raise costs without support can push the burden onto the households least
able to absorb it, so access should be designed into the intervention.
