## What a footprint is

A carbon footprint expresses the greenhouse gases associated with a person's or
institution's activity as a single comparable figure, usually in kilograms or
tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e). "Equivalent" matters: methane and
nitrous oxide have far greater warming effect per unit than CO2, so they are
converted to a common scale.

## Where the emissions sit

For most individuals the dominant categories are transport, household energy,
food and goods. The usual finding is that a small number of behaviours account
for most of the total — frequent long-distance flying, daily private car
commuting, and heating or cooling a poorly insulated space. This concentration
is useful: it means a few changes move the number far more than a long list of
small ones.

## The high-leverage changes

**Travel less, and travel differently.** Shifting a regular commute from a
private car to public transport, cycling or walking typically produces the
largest single reduction available to an individual, because it removes fuel
combustion entirely rather than reducing it slightly.

**Electrify and decarbonise the home.** Improving insulation and draught
proofing reduces demand before any equipment is replaced. Where the grid is
becoming cleaner, switching heating and cooking to electricity lowers lifetime
emissions further.

**Shift the diet.** Food accounts for a substantial share of household
emissions. Reducing the frequency of ruminant meat meals, and cutting food
waste, both reduce emissions without requiring a wholesale change in diet.

**Buy fewer, longer-lived things.** Manufacturing dominates the footprint of
most durable goods, so extending a product's life — repair, resale, sharing —
is usually more effective than choosing a marginally greener new version.

## What does not help much

Small symbolic substitutions in isolation rarely move the total. The value of a
change depends on the emissions it displaces, not on how visible it is.

## Uncertainty and honesty

Footprints are estimates built from average factors, so they are useful for
comparing options and useless as precise measurements. Treat any figure as a
direction and a magnitude, never as an exact score.

## Collective action still matters

Individual footprints sit inside shared infrastructure — grid mix, transit
provision, building standards. Reducing personal emissions and advocating for
cleaner systems are complements, not alternatives.
