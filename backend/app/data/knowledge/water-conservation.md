## Why water efficiency matters

Fresh water is renewable but finite in any given place and season. When demand
outpaces recharge, aquifers fall and rivers run low, which in turn raises
pumping costs and energy use. Saving water therefore usually saves energy as
well, since treating and moving water is one of the largest municipal
electricity loads.

## Where water actually goes

In most buildings the largest uses are, in rough order: flushing, bathing,
laundry, kitchen and outdoor watering. Leaks are the quiet outlier — a single
dripping tap or a running flapper valve can waste more in a month than a
household's deliberate conservation saves.

## Measures that work

**Find leaks first.** Read the meter before and after a two-hour period with no
water use. Any movement indicates a leak. This one check routinely outperforms
every other intervention.

**Fit the flow, not the habit.** Low-flow aerators, dual-flush or displacement
devices and water-efficient showerheads reduce use without changing behaviour.

**Change the default.** Sweep instead of hosing paved areas; water plants in the
early morning or evening; use a bucket and mug rather than an open tap.

**Harvest and reuse.** Rainwater collected from roofs is well suited to flushing
and gardening. Greywater from rinsing can be reused for non-potable purposes
where local rules permit.

**Design for the landscape.** Native and drought-adapted planting, mulch and
contoured beds cut irrigation demand sharply because they hold soil moisture.

## Institutions and campuses

Sub-metering buildings makes consumption visible and is usually the fastest way
to find the anomaly. Restricting irrigation to timed cycles, installing sensor
taps in shared washrooms, and repairing reported leaks within a stated service
window turn a policy into a routine.

## Measuring progress

Track litres per person per day or per square metre of building, not total
consumption — an institution that grows should not be rewarded for a flat line.

## A note on fairness

Water saved in one place is not automatically available in another. Local
catchment conditions, seasonal recharge and downstream users all shape what
"enough" means, so conservation targets should be set with the local water
authority rather than applied uniformly.
