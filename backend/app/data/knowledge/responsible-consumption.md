## Reduce the number of decisions

The most durable sustainable habits are not the most virtuous ones — they are
the ones that remove a decision. A refillable bottle in the bag means no
single-use bottle is ever considered. A repair kit at home means replacement is
not the default. Designing the context beats relying on willpower.

## Single-use items

Bottles, bags, cups and cutlery are convenient precisely because they are
designed to be discarded, and their impact is dominated by production and
disposal rather than use. The largest gains come from substitution at a fixed
point in the day: the commute, the canteen, the shop. Where substitution is
awkward, the reusable option usually needs to be carried, available and clean —
so convenience is part of the design, not a compromise with it.

## Purchasing decisions

Impact concentrates in what is bought and how long it lasts:

- **Durability and repairability** — a longer-lived item almost always beats a
  marginally greener short-lived one.
- **Second-hand and refurbished** — avoids new manufacturing entirely.
- **Repair over replace** — often cheaper than assumed, and increasingly
  supported by service networks and right-to-repair rules.
- **Shared access rather than ownership** where use is occasional — tools,
  equipment, vehicles.
- **Fewer, better purchases** rather than substituting between similar options.

## Plastics and packaging

Recycling is valuable but partial: not all plastics are mechanically recyclable,
and the number of recycling cycles is limited. Reducing packaging at the point
of purchase — loose produce, refill stations, concentrated products — removes
the material before it exists.

## Avoiding the performance of sustainability

The least useful pattern is a high-visibility gesture paired with unchanged
underlying throughput. Because production dominates most product footprints,
buying fewer things is more consequential than choosing between similar ones.

## What organisations can change

Procurement specifications, refill and repair facilities, reusable serviceware,
and honest labelling at the point of decision all shift behaviour far more
reliably than awareness campaigns alone.
