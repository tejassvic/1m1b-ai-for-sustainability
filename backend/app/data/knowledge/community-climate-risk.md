## The shape of the problem

Climate risk is not only a matter of hazards becoming more severe. It is the
product of a hazard meeting an exposed and vulnerable community: where people
live, how buildings are made, who has resources to move or recover, and how
quickly warnings reach them. Two places with identical rainfall can suffer very
different outcomes.

## The four-part cycle

Community climate resilience is normally organised as a continuing cycle rather
than a one-off project:

1. **Understand** — map the hazards that actually affect the area, and who and
   what is exposed to them.
2. **Prepare** — reduce vulnerability through land-use rules, drainage,
   building practice, early-warning systems and stored supplies.
3. **Respond** — agree in advance who acts, where people go, and how the warning
   travels, including to those without phones.
4. **Recover and learn** — review what failed after each event and adjust.

## Practical measures

**Local hazard knowledge.** Communities often hold detail that no dataset
contains: which lane floods first, which slope moves after heavy rain, which
bridge fails. Recording this formally makes it usable.

**Early warning that reaches everyone.** Warnings only reduce harm when they are
timely, trusted and actionable. That means more than an SMS gateway: agreed
thresholds, familiar messengers, and clear instructions on what to do.

**Protective infrastructure and maintenance.** Drain clearance, check dams,
retaining structures and revegetated slopes are cheap compared with repair after
failure, provided someone is responsible for upkeep.

**Diversified livelihoods and savings.** The ability to absorb a bad season is
itself a resilience measure.

**Inclusive planning.** Those most exposed — outdoor workers, older residents,
people with disabilities, informal settlements — are frequently absent from the
planning conversation and most affected by its gaps.

## Working with, not against, nature

Ecosystem-based measures such as mangrove belts, wetland restoration, urban
tree canopy and hillslope afforestation reduce hazard intensity while providing
other benefits. They are usually slower than engineering but cheaper, longer
lived and self-maintaining once established.

## Honest communication

Risk estimates describe likelihood under stated assumptions. They do not
predict a specific event, and a low estimate is not a guarantee of safety —
particularly where historical data is sparse. Guidance should say so plainly.
